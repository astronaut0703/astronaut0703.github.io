For the first time, I won an award at a CTF. And it was the grand prize. I didn't climb there slowly, through honorable mention, excellence, and top excellence awards. The very first award I ever received was the grand prize.

But when the competition ended, all I felt was empty. Do I deserve this award? What is it even good for? Does using a better AI model, or simply being luckier, make me worthy of the grand prize?

## Before the award

Looking back, before I won I felt ashamed. These days awards and CVEs seem to pour out everywhere, and I had none of them. I felt like I was falling behind. Somewhere in the back of my mind, I believed I needed "that thing" everyone else seemed to have in order to grow in the security industry.

## After the award

But once I actually won, I couldn't confidently say that I had grown. I finally held the "thing" I had wanted so badly, yet I didn't feel much different from who I was before. I think I understood, a little, why so many people quit CTFs.

## What I still learned

That doesn't mean I gained nothing from this competition. There are things I clearly learned.

If you try to solve CTF challenges with AI alone, knowing nothing about security, it can take a very long time or produce no results at all. This probably applies not only to CTFs but to bug finding in general, including bug bounties. (As you might tell from my earlier blog posts, I had recently been asking myself, "How on earth do people solve challenges and earn bug bounties with AI?" while searching for what was wrong with me, given my poor results.)

A good model alone is not enough. You need to set up your AI tooling for the purpose in advance, make the most of the background security knowledge relevant to that purpose, and combine program analysis techniques such as fuzzing and symbolic execution with AI techniques. And on top of that, plenty of tokens.

## The remaining question, and what comes next

Going back to the original question, it is still hard to say that this award proves I have grown.

Instead, this competition showed me what I need to dig into further: applying security knowledge and program analysis techniques, and how to "use" AI on top of them. This may sound somewhat abstract, but I believe that if I keep developing it, it can be applied, and will be needed, not only in bug finding but also in security research.
